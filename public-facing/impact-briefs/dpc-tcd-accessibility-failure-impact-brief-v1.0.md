# IMPACT BRIEF — DPC Handling of TCD Complaint & Failure to Accommodate

Trauma‑Informed Systems Design • Regulatory Conduct Analysis • September 2026

---

I. Executive Summary

The Data Protection Commission’s (DPC) handling of Borealis S. Hedling’s complaints—both the Article 16 GDPR complaint against Trinity College Dublin (TCD) and the Equal Status Act complaint against the DPC itself—demonstrates systemic failures in accessibility, trauma‑informed practice, and compliance with international human rights obligations.

The DPC’s 28 September 2026 letter mischaracterizes the procedural history, incorrectly treats a duress‑induced withdrawal as voluntary, and applies domestic procedural timelines in circumstances where international law requires flexibility, urgency, and accommodation.

The cumulative impact is a regulatory environment that re‑traumatizes, misinterprets disability‑related communication patterns, and obscures the substantive rights violations at issue.

---

II. Key Findings

1. Misclassification of a duress‑induced withdrawal as voluntary
The DPC asserts that the complaint against TCD was withdrawn.  
Documentary evidence shows the withdrawal occurred during:

- acute duress,  
- fragmentation caused by torture and transnational repression,  
- disability‑related communication impairment,  
- absence of the requested accommodation.

Under the Disability Act 2005 and CRPD Article 12, such a withdrawal cannot be treated as informed or voluntary.

Impact:  
The DPC’s interpretation erases the disability context and undermines procedural fairness.

---

2. Failure to provide the requested accommodation (in‑person filing)
On 26 April 2026, Borealis explicitly requested:

> “to exercise my rights under the 2005 Disability Act to file a complaint… in person.”

The DPC:

- did not acknowledge the request,  
- did not provide alternatives,  
- did not engage the Accessibility Officer,  
- and later used the absence of accommodation as evidence that accommodation was not needed.

Impact:  
This constitutes a breach of:

- Disability Act 2005 (reasonable accommodation),  
- Equal Status Acts (discrimination on disability grounds),  
- CRPD Articles 13 & 21 (access to justice & communication),  
- UNCAT Article 13 (obligation to accompany victims of torture).

---

3. Misinterpretation of fragmentation as inconsistency
The DPC’s letter treats fragmented communication as evidence of procedural irregularity.

However, fragmentation is:

- a known trauma response,  
- a disability‑related communication impairment,  
- exacerbated by torture, homelessness, and lack of medical care.

Impact:  
The DPC’s analysis pathologizes the victim rather than recognizing the impairment, resulting in:

- misinterpretation of intent,  
- misclassification of procedural steps,  
- erosion of trust in regulatory processes.

---

4. Improper reliance on ES1 timelines and WRC procedural expectations
The DPC repeatedly cites:

- ES1 timelines,  
- “reasonable time to respond,”  
- WRC procedural norms.

These are not applicable in contexts involving:

- torture,  
- transnational repression,  
- disability‑related communication barriers,  
- international human rights obligations.

Under Irish law and international law, ES1 timelines can be displaced when:

- the complainant is under duress,  
- the complainant has a disability,  
- the respondent has already breached statutory duties,  
- international obligations supersede domestic procedure.

Impact:  
The DPC’s procedural framing is legally insufficient and incompatible with:

- UNCAT Articles 12 & 13,  
- CRPD Articles 13 & 21,  
- EU Charter of Fundamental Rights,  
- GDPR Article 12 (promptness).

---

5. Failure to address the substantive Article 16 GDPR issue
The DPC’s letter reframes the Article 16 request as an attempt to “appeal” TCD’s decision.

This is incorrect.

The Article 16 request sought:

- rectification of incomplete personal data,  
- supplemental information noting victimisation,  
- correction of inaccurate data in the exclusion letter.

Impact:  
The DPC’s misinterpretation prevents meaningful examination of:

- data accuracy,  
- completeness,  
- fairness,  
- and the victimisation context.

---

III. Systemic Implications

A. Regulatory Capture Risk
The DPC’s deference to institutional narratives (TCD, HSE, IPAT, etc.) mirrors patterns of regulatory capture in trauma‑producing systems.

B. Accessibility Failure
The DPC’s Accessibility Officer did not intervene despite clear indicators of disability‑related communication barriers.

C. International Law Non‑Compliance
The DPC’s approach is incompatible with:

- UNCAT (Ireland is a State Party),  
- CRPD (Ireland is a State Party),  
- EU GDPR obligations,  
- EU Charter obligations.

D. Re‑traumatization
The DPC’s procedural rigidity and misinterpretation of duress exacerbate trauma and undermine trust.

---

IV. Recommendations

1. Reinstate and continue the TCD complaint
Recognize the withdrawal as invalid due to duress and disability.

2. Reopen the complaint against the DPC
Address failures to accommodate and procedural unfairness.

3. Engage the Accessibility Officer immediately
Provide oral or alternative communication channels.

4. Apply international human rights obligations
Supersede ES1 timelines where torture, duress, or disability are present.

5. Conduct a trauma‑informed review of all correspondence
Reassess fragmentation as a disability‑related communication pattern.

---

V. Conclusion

The DPC’s handling of these complaints reflects systemic failures in accessibility, trauma‑informed practice, and compliance with international human rights obligations. The impact is not merely procedural—it is human, affecting safety, dignity, and the ability to exercise fundamental rights.

A trauma‑informed, rights‑based correction is required.
