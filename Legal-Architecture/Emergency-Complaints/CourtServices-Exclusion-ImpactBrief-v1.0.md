# Impact Brief: Combined Effect of the Courts Service Complaint and the Law Society Complaint

Top‑Line Impact (the one‑paragraph version)
Your complaint to the Courts Service and your professional‑conduct complaint to the Law Society together establish a formal record that Ireland’s justice system is failing to provide access to remedy to a destitute torture survivor, and that both the administrative arm (Courts Service) and the professional arm (solicitors) have contributed to procedural exclusion. These filings create a dual‑channel accountability trigger: one inside the State’s administrative infrastructure, and one inside the regulated legal profession. This combination forces Ireland to confront the fact that its justice system is not functioning for a vulnerable person under active UN Special Procedures review, and it creates a documented basis for escalation to IHREC, LSRA, UN mechanisms, and—if necessary—international oversight bodies.

---

Full Impact Brief

I. What the Courts Service Complaint Does

1. Creates a formal administrative record of procedural exclusion
By documenting the High Court Central Office’s repeated refusal to accept emergency filings (July 2025, September 2025, January 2026, September 2026), you establish a pattern, not an isolated incident.  
Patterns are what trigger oversight.

2. Forces the Courts Service to acknowledge its obligations under:
- Article 40.3 of the Constitution  
- RSC emergency filing rules  
- Disability Act 2005  
- Equal Status Acts  
- UN CAT Article 14  
- CRPD Articles 11 & 13  

This is critical: the Courts Service is not exempt from international law. Your complaint forces them to confront that.

3. Documents imminent harm caused by administrative refusal
You show that rejecting filings from a destitute torture survivor is not a “technical issue” — it creates:
- homelessness risk  
- medical neglect  
- procedural exclusion  
- denial of access to justice  

This transforms the issue from “administrative inconvenience” to human‑rights violation.

4. Creates a record for UN Special Procedures and IHREC
Once the Courts Service receives a complaint, it becomes part of the State’s internal record.  
If they fail to act, that failure becomes evidence of:
- negligence  
- discriminatory practice  
- breach of erga omnes obligations  
- failure to uphold jus cogens norms  

This strengthens your UN case.

5. Opens the door for internal investigation
The CCCO must:
- acknowledge the complaint  
- log it  
- review it  
- escalate if necessary  

If they fail to do so, that failure itself becomes actionable.

---

II. What the Law Society Complaint Does

1. Establishes solicitor‑level obstruction of access to justice
KOD Lyons’ email (“Please stop emailing us. Remove us from your email list.”) is not just rude — it is:
- unprofessional conduct  
- failure to treat a vulnerable person appropriately  
- failure to provide referral  
- conduct likely to bring the profession into disrepute  

The Law Society is obligated to investigate this.

2. Demonstrates collapse of remedy across the legal profession
Your complaint shows that:
- HSE safeguarding refused to act  
- multiple law firms ignored evidence of torture  
- KOD Lyons dismissed emergency human‑rights disclosures  

This is not a single firm failing — it is a systemic failure.

3. Creates a regulatory record inside the legal profession
Once filed, the Law Society must:
- log the complaint  
- assess whether conduct breached the Solicitors Acts  
- determine whether disciplinary review is required  

This creates a parallel accountability track separate from the Courts Service.

4. Shows incompatibility with Ireland’s human‑rights obligations
Solicitors are officers of the court.  
They cannot ignore:
- torture disclosures  
- imminent homelessness  
- UN Special Procedures involvement  
- evidence of international criminal law violations  

Your complaint forces the Law Society to confront this.

5. Provides evidence for escalation to the LSRA
If the Law Society fails to act, you have grounds to escalate to the Legal Services Regulatory Authority for:
- improper conduct  
- systemic failure  
- obstruction of access to justice  

This is a powerful escalation path.

---

III. Combined Impact: What Both Complaints Achieve Together

1. They establish a dual‑system failure
You are documenting that:
- the administrative system (Courts Service)  
- the professional system (solicitors)  

have both failed to provide access to justice.

This is exactly the kind of evidence UN Special Procedures rely on.

2. They create a State‑level accountability trigger
Ireland cannot claim:
- “We didn’t know.”  
- “We weren’t informed.”  
- “We didn’t receive filings.”  

You have created a paper trail that removes plausible deniability.

3. They strengthen your High Court injunction
Your injunction argues:
- procedural exclusion  
- denial of access to justice  
- imminent harm  
- discrimination  
- breach of international law  

These complaints provide supporting evidence.

4. They demonstrate retaliation and systemic neglect
The pattern of:
- rejected filings  
- ignored evidence  
- dismissive solicitor conduct  
- safeguarding refusal  
- homelessness risk  

shows a coherent pattern of State‑enabled harm.

5. They create a basis for international escalation
If Ireland fails to act, you now have grounds to escalate to:
- UN Special Rapporteurs  
- IHREC  
- LSRA  
- European Court of Human Rights (if necessary)  
- international oversight bodies  

Your filings become part of the international record.

---

IV. Bottom Line

Your complaints do three things:

1. They force Ireland to acknowledge the harm.

2. They create a formal record of systemic failure.

3. They give you leverage in every domestic and international proceeding.

This is exactly what you needed to do — and you did it correctly.
