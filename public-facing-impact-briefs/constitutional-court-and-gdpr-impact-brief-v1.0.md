I. Overview
In September 2026, Borealis S. Hedling submitted two major filings to the institutions of the Principality of Liechtenstein:

1. A Constitutional Court Petition (Staatsgerichtshof)  
2. A GDPR Article‑16 Request for Rectification of Parliamentary Records

Together, these filings transform a moment of public humiliation in the Liechtenstein Landtag into a documented human‑rights event with legal, institutional, and international implications.

This brief explains what was done, why it matters, and what the impact is.

---

II. What Was Submitted

1. Constitutional Court Petition (Staatsgerichtshof)
The petition asks the Constitutional Court to examine:

- violations of human dignity (Art. 27bis, Liechtenstein Constitution)  
- denial of justice  
- discrimination  
- failure to provide disability‑related procedural accommodations  
- the role of Liechtenstein in a multi‑state chain of harm involving the US, Canada, Iceland, Switzerland, Ireland, and Liechtenstein itself  

The petition consolidates years of documented torture, refoulement, unlawful detention, and procedural humiliation into a single, unified case.

Key impact:
It forces the Court to treat the harm as constitutional, not administrative.

---

2. GDPR Article‑16 Rectification Request
This request demands correction of inaccurate and discriminatory personal data in the official Landtag transcript of 7 May 2025, including:

- misgendering  
- deadnaming  
- unfounded mental‑health insinuations  
- mischaracterization of petition content  

These statements were made publicly by MP Sandra Fausch and recorded in the parliamentary minutes.

Key impact:
It reframes humiliation as unlawful data processing, triggering mandatory GDPR obligations.

III. Why These Filings Matter

1. They transform humiliation into evidence
The Landtag’s statements are no longer “political remarks.”  
They are now:

- inaccurate personal data  
- discriminatory processing  
- dignity violations  
- constitutional harms  
- GDPR‑regulated records  

This changes the status of the event from political dismissal to documented rights violation.

2. They create dual accountability tracks
Liechtenstein must now respond through:

- constitutional law (Staatsgerichtshof)  
- data protection law (GDPR)  

These two systems operate independently.  
Avoiding one does not avoid the other.

3. They force Liechtenstein into international visibility
The filings trigger obligations under:

- ECHR  
- CRPD  
- UN Declaration on Human Rights Defenders  
- GANHRI membership standards

Micro‑states prefer to operate quietly.
