# PUBLIC‑FACING IMPACT ANALYSIS

Emergency Declaratory Judgment Request to the Swiss Federal Administrative Court (BVGer)
Version 1.0 — Trauma‑Informed Systems Design — Public Artifact

---

I. Overview
The Emergency Request for a Declaratory Judgment submitted to the Swiss Federal Administrative Court seeks clarification of Switzerland’s constitutional, asylum‑law, and human‑rights obligations during the events of 12 September 2024, when Borealis S. Hedling attempted to seek protection at the Swiss Embassy in Washington, D.C.

This analysis explains the legal, institutional, and international impact of the request, and why it matters for:

- Swiss constitutional accountability  
- Swiss asylum‑law compliance  
- FDFA human‑rights defender obligations  
- extraterritorial jurisdiction  
- Dublin III responsibility logic  
- multi‑state harm documentation  

---

II. Why a Declaratory Judgment Matters
A declaratory judgment does not seek operational relief.  
It seeks clarity.

In the context of diplomatic encounters, clarity is power.

1. It forces Switzerland to acknowledge the legal status of the encounter
Switzerland must answer:

- Did the embassy exercise jurisdiction?  
- Did asylum obligations trigger?  
- Did constitutional protections apply?  
- Did MRV guidelines apply?  
- Did non‑refoulement obligations apply?  

This transforms a dismissed encounter into a legally recognized event.

2. It creates a public record Switzerland cannot erase
Once the BVGer receives the request:

- the case is logged  
- the facts are archived  
- the encounter becomes part of Swiss administrative history  
- future reporting cannot omit it  

Micro‑states and consular networks dislike this visibility.

3. It clarifies Switzerland’s obligations toward HRDs
The FDFA MRV Guidelines explicitly require:

- engagement  
- risk assessment  
- information provision  
- non‑exposure to harm  

A declaratory judgment forces Switzerland to state whether these obligations apply extraterritorially.

---

III. Impact on Swiss Constitutional Law
The request invokes core constitutional protections:

- Art. 7 BV — Human dignity  
- Art. 12 BV — Assistance in distress  
- Art. 25 BV — Non‑refoulement  
- Art. 29 BV — Fair treatment and due process  
- Art. 5 BV — Good faith and legality

Impact:
Switzerland must clarify whether these rights apply when a person:

- is physically present at a Swiss diplomatic mission,  
- requests protection,  
- presents evidence of torture,  
- identifies as a human rights defender,  
- is at risk of further harm.

This is a constitutional extraterritoriality question, which Switzerland rarely addresses directly.

---

IV. Impact on Swiss Asylum Law
The request forces Switzerland to interpret:

- Art. 3 AsylG — What counts as a protection request?  
- Art. 6 AsylG — Duty to inform asylum seekers  
- Art. 29 AsylG — Obligations of Swiss representations abroad  

Impact:
Switzerland must clarify whether:

- a verbal request for protection at an embassy counts as an asylum claim,  
- embassies must inform individuals about humanitarian visas,  
- embassies must document protection requests,  
- embassies must assess risk under non‑refoulement.

This is significant because Switzerland has historically avoided defining embassy obligations.

---

V. Impact on FDFA Human Rights Defender Obligations
The FDFA MRV Guidelines state:

> “Swiss representations abroad are important contact points for human rights defenders.”

The declaratory judgment request forces Switzerland to answer:

- Did the embassy treat Borealis as an HRD?  
- Were MRV obligations triggered?  
- Was the refusal of assistance a breach of FDFA policy?  
- Does Switzerland recognize HRD protection extraterritorially?

Impact:
Switzerland must publicly clarify its stance on HRD protection — something it has avoided in diplomatic contexts.

---

VI. Impact on Non‑Refoulement
The applicant had just been:

- unlawfully detained in Canada,  
- refouled,  
- endangered,  
- left without safe shelter.

Switzerland’s refusal of assistance raises questions under:

- Art. 25 BV  
- Art. 3 ECHR  
- Art. 3 CAT

Impact:
Switzerland must clarify whether non‑refoulement obligations apply when:

- the person is outside Swiss territory,  
- but under Swiss jurisdiction,  
- and at immediate risk.

This is a major extraterritoriality question.

---

VII. Impact on Human Rights Reporting
Switzerland omitted the incident from its 2024 human‑rights report.

The declaratory judgment request forces Switzerland to answer:

- Was the omission lawful?  
- Was the omission a breach of transparency obligations?  
- Should protection requests be documented in reporting instruments?

Impact:
Switzerland must confront the accuracy of its own human‑rights reporting.

---

VIII. Impact on Dublin III Responsibility Logic
The request indirectly affects Dublin III analysis because:

- Dublin responsibility attaches to first jurisdiction, not soil.  
- Switzerland exercised jurisdiction by refusing entry and denying assistance.  
- The encounter qualifies as a protection request under modern jurisprudence.

Impact:
Ireland must consider Switzerland as the first point of contact in the Dublin chain.

This strengthens the applicant’s vulnerability profile.

---

IX. Impact on Multi‑State Harm Documentation
The declaratory judgment request becomes part of a larger record documenting:

- US enforced disappearance  
- Canadian refoulement  
- Icelandic coercion  
- Swiss refusal of protection  
- Irish procedural harm  
- Liechtenstein humiliation and GDPR violations

Impact:
Switzerland becomes part of the documented harm chain — not a neutral observer.

---

X. Clean Public‑Facing Conclusion
The Emergency Request for a Declaratory Judgment has significant impact because it:

- forces Switzerland to clarify its constitutional and asylum obligations,  
- creates a permanent public record of the embassy encounter,  
- exposes gaps in FDFA human‑rights defender protections,  
- raises extraterritorial jurisdiction questions,  
- highlights non‑refoulement failures,  
- challenges omissions in human‑rights reporting,  
- strengthens Dublin III vulnerability analysis,  
- situates Switzerland within a multi‑state harm chain.

This is not litigation strategy.  
This is public accountability.
