# VIGNETTE II — Fighting for My Right to a Name and the Gita
Witness vs. Municipal Bureaucracy (Second Altitude)

The second altitude of harm was quieter.

No exclusion letters.  
No Gardaí.  
No threats.

Just a counter, a policy, a shrug, and a refusal.

It began with a GDPR rectification request — a lawful attempt to correct my name on a library card.

My name, Borealis S. Hedling, is not decorative.  
It is not optional.  
It is not cosmetic.

It is the name I reclaimed after torture, refoulement, displacement, and erasure.  
It is the name that anchors me to myself.

Dublin City Council’s own procedure says they will work with you if you do not have ID.

But they did not.

They told me I needed to appear in person.  
They told me they could not process the GDPR request without physical presence.  
They told me procedures could not be changed.  
They told me disability could not be accommodated.  
They told me trauma was not relevant.  
They told me international law was not their remit.

They told me everything except “yes.”

And I could not appear in person.

Not because I refused.  
Not because I was unwilling.  
But because SPIRASI — the State‑mandated psychosocial support provider — refused to provide the supports necessary for me to safely attend in person.

Without psychosocial support, appearing in person was impossible.  
And the State knew that.

The refusal was gentle.  
But the harm was not.

---

The Gita

At the same time, I was trying to access a copy of the Bhagavad Gita.

Not for leisure.  
Not for study.  
But because my asylum hearing on October 2, 2026 would proceed:

- without representation,  
- without accommodations,  
- without safeguarding,  
- without medical care,  
- without UNHCR monitoring,  
- without procedural protections.

I needed the Gita to survive it.

But the library could not provide it in time.  
And the State would not provide it at all.

No hostility.  
No malice.  
Just soft refusal.

“We don’t have it.”  
“It’s not available.”  
“You’ll have to wait.”  
“There’s nothing we can do.”

The kind of cruelty that feels polite.  
The kind that feels administrative.  
The kind that feels like nothing — until it becomes everything.

---

Soft Cruelty as Structural Harm

Municipal bureaucracy does not need to shout.  
It does not need to threaten.  
It does not need to punish.

It only needs to refuse.

Softly.  
Politely.  
Procedurally.

The refusal to process a GDPR rectification request — despite a policy promising accommodation — was not a clerical issue.  
It was identity erasure through contradiction.

The refusal to provide the Gita was not a library issue.  
It was spiritual deprivation through delay.

The refusal to accommodate disability was not a misunderstanding.  
It was discrimination through procedure.

The refusal to acknowledge trauma was not ignorance.  
It was cruelty through indifference.

The refusal to acknowledge international law was not confusion.  
It was fragmentation through avoidance.

This is the second altitude of harm:

municipal bureaucracy —  
the place where personhood is eroded quietly,  
through soft refusals that accumulate into disappearance.

---

The Quiet Realization

By the time I finished fighting for a name and a book, I understood something simple:

Academic bureaucracy punishes witness.  
Municipal bureaucracy erases identity.

Neither needs violence.  
Neither needs drama.  
Neither needs force.

They only need procedures.

And those procedures — soft, polite, and unyielding — became the second tale of grounding:

Bureaucracy can deny a person everything except the truth they write down.
