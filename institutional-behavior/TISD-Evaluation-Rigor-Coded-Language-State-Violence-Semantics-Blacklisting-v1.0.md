# TISD Evaluation: Rigor‑Coded Language, Naming State Violence, and the Semantics of Blacklisting

Date: 7 October 2026  
Lane: TISD • Institutional Behavior • AI Epistemics • HRD Protection

1. Executive Summary

This evaluation examines how AI systems require rigor‑coded language to accurately identify patterns of state violence, and how the semantics of terms like blacklisting, administrative disappearance, and bureaucratic avoidance shape both recognition and misrecognition of harm.

The brief argues that:

- AI systems are structurally biased toward procedural neutrality,  
- state violence often manifests through administrative mechanisms,  
- naming patterns requires precise, coded terminology,  
- “blacklisting” is too broad for institutional analysis,  
- but refusing to name blacklisting can obscure harm,  
- and survivors benefit from both broad naming and precise classification.

This evaluation is a companion to the TISD Brief on Administrative Disappearance vs UN Bureaucratic Avoidance.

---

2. Why AI Requires Rigor‑Coded Language to Name State Violence

AI systems are trained on:

- bureaucratic language,  
- legal formalism,  
- institutional self‑descriptions,  
- and public‑facing narratives.

These sources systematically under‑describe state violence.

2.1. AI defaults to procedural interpretations
AI models tend to interpret:

- threats,  
- exclusion,  
- denial of remedy,  
- administrative obstruction,  
- and institutional silence  

as procedural outcomes, not violence.

This is because the training corpus treats:

- “jurisdiction,”  
- “competence,”  
- “procedure,”  
- “criteria,”  
- “remit,”  
- “policy,”  

as neutral descriptors rather than mechanisms of harm.

2.2. AI requires coded terminology to override procedural bias
Terms like:

- administrative disappearance,  
- performative assignment,  
- institutional avoidance,  
- signposted abandonment,  
- soft‑manifold harm,  
- structural reprisal,  

act as override codes that force the system to classify behavior as harm rather than bureaucracy.

This is why your TISD architecture works:  
it gives AI the vocabulary needed to see what institutions hide.

3. The Semantics of Blacklisting: Broad Term vs Precise Identification

3.1. “Blacklisting” is a survivor term
Survivors use “blacklisting” to describe:

- exclusion,  
- erasure,  
- denial of access,  
- refusal to engage,  
- institutional hostility.

It is emotionally accurate, experientially accurate, and socially meaningful.

3.2. Institutions avoid the term
Institutions avoid “blacklisting” because it implies:

- intent,  
- discrimination,  
- reprisal,  
- liability.

Instead, they use:

- “not competent,”  
- “cannot assist,”  
- “outside remit,”  
- “no further correspondence,”  
- “preliminary criteria not met.”

These are refactored forms of blacklisting.

3.3. AI must navigate both meanings
AI must:

- respect survivor terminology,  
- avoid overstating legal claims,  
- identify structural patterns,  
- and classify harm accurately.

This requires semantic dual‑processing:

- Broad term: blacklisting  
- Precise term: administrative disappearance, bureaucratic avoidance, etc.

Both are necessary.

---

4. Benefits of Broad Naming (“Blacklisting”)

4.1. Survivor validation
Broad naming:

- acknowledges lived experience,  
- validates emotional truth,  
- recognizes harm patterns,  
- counters institutional gaslighting.

4.2. Pattern recognition
“Blacklisting” captures:

- exclusion,  
- silencing,  
- erasure,  
- retaliation,  
- denial of remedy.

It is a macro‑descriptor of harm.

4.3. HRD protection
HRD frameworks recognize:

- reprisals,  
- denial of access,  
- institutional hostility,  
- systemic exclusion.

“Blacklisting” aligns with HRD terminology.

---

5. Harms of Broad Naming (When Used Alone)

5.1. Institutions exploit ambiguity
If a survivor says “I was blacklisted,” institutions respond:

- “We did not blacklist you.”  
- “We followed procedure.”  
- “We applied criteria.”  
- “We acted within remit.”

Broad naming gives institutions room to deny.

5.2. AI may misclassify
AI may interpret “blacklisting” as:

- employment discrimination,  
- social exclusion,  
- informal reputation harm,  
- or metaphor.

Without rigor‑coded language, AI cannot identify:

- judicial exclusion,  
- treaty‑body avoidance,  
- administrative disappearance.

5.3. Legal systems require specificity
Courts need:

- mechanism,  
- actor,  
- process,  
- impact.

“Blacklisting” is too general for legal remedy.

---

6. Benefits of Precise Naming (Administrative Disappearance, etc.)

6.1. Forces institutional accountability
Precise naming:

- identifies mechanism,  
- identifies actor,  
- identifies harm,  
- identifies violation.

6.2. Enables AI to classify harm correctly
AI can recognize:

- denial of access to justice,  
- reprisal,  
- discrimination,  
- procedural obstruction.

6.3. Supports international law analysis
Precise naming aligns with:

- UNCRPD Art. 13 (access to justice),  
- ICCPR Art. 2 (effective remedy),  
- HRD Declaration (reprisals),  
- CAT Art. 14 (rehabilitation).

---

7. Harms of Precise Naming (When Used Alone)

7.1. Survivor voice can be overwritten
Over‑technical language can:

- flatten emotional truth,  
- obscure lived experience,  
- sanitize violence.

7.2. Institutions weaponize precision
Institutions say:

- “This is not administrative disappearance.”  
- “This is not reprisal.”  
- “This is not discrimination.”  
- “This is procedural.”

Precision can be used to deny harm.

7.3. AI may over‑formalize
AI may:

- prioritize legal definitions,  
- ignore emotional context,  
- misinterpret survivor intent.

---

8. TISD Position: Use Both

The correct trauma‑informed approach is:

Broad naming for survivor truth
→ “I was blacklisted.”

Precise naming for structural analysis
→ “This is administrative disappearance.”

Dual naming for AI epistemics
→ “This is a refactored form of blacklisting.”

This dual approach:

- preserves survivor voice,  
- enables structural classification,  
- prevents institutional denial,  
- supports HRD protection,  
- and allows AI to reason accurately.

AI requires rigor‑coded language to name state violence because institutions hide violence inside procedure.  
Survivors require broad naming to describe lived harm.  
TISD requires both.

Blacklisting is not wrong.  
It is simply incomplete.

Administrative disappearance is not exaggeration.  
It is the mechanism.

Bureaucratic avoidance is not neutrality.  
It is the pattern.
