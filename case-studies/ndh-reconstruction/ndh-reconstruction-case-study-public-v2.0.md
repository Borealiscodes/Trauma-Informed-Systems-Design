# **NDH Reconstruction Case Study (Public‑Facing Edition)**  
### *How a distributed research ecosystem rebuilt itself across 12 repositories and nearly 7,000 commits*

---

## **Overview**

Over the past months, the NDH ecosystem has gone through a major reconstruction.  
This work spanned:

- **12+ repositories**  
- **6,981 commits**  
- multiple design eras  
- several stability engines  
- an expressive ecology  
- and a full governance rewrite  

This case study explains **how** NDH rebuilt itself, **why** the process was nonlinear, and **what changed** as the system matured.

It is written for a general audience — researchers, designers, and curious readers — who want to understand the evolution of a complex, human‑centered system without needing to know its internal mathematics.

---

## **1. Why the reconstruction wasn’t linear**

NDH didn’t grow like a traditional software project.

It grew more like an **ecosystem**:

- different parts evolved at different times  
- stability tools appeared before formal theory  
- expressive elements emerged before governance  
- and new insights reshaped older structures  

This meant the reconstruction had to follow the **actual path the system took**, not a tidy chronological order.

Think of it like restoring a forest:  
you don’t rebuild it tree‑by‑tree — you rebuild it **pattern‑by‑pattern**.

---

## **2. Why reconstruction became necessary**

By mid‑2026, NDH’s early simulation tools were producing:

- too much conceptual load  
- too many overlapping structures  
- and too much activity across too many repositories  

The system was growing fast — **over 5,200 commits in a short window**, then accelerating to **6,981**.

This wasn’t a problem of “too much code.”  
It was a problem of **too much conceptual curvature** — too many ideas bending the system in different directions at once.

To protect the human‑centered goals of the project, NDH needed:

- clearer governance  
- safer design rules  
- better decompression tools  
- and a unified architecture  

This is where Trauma‑Informed‑Systems‑Design (TISD) stepped in.

---

## **3. The role of Surfboard v2.0 (updated)**

Surfboard v2.0 was the **first stability tool** NDH ever built.

It came from the early simulation era and was designed to:

- keep the system balanced  
- prevent runaway complexity  
- protect identity and continuity  
- and help users stay grounded during intense conceptual work  

It was created **before** NDH had its later mathematical tools, expressive ecology, or governance frameworks.

Surfboard v2.0 was the “bridge” between:

- early experimentation  
- and later structured design  

It didn’t solve every problem — but it kept the system stable long enough for the next generation of tools to emerge.

---

## **4. How Surfboard v2.0 led to Unified Companion Maps**

As NDH grew, Surfboard v2.0 wasn’t enough.

The system needed:

- clearer structure  
- safer transitions  
- better ways to connect ideas  
- and a way to unify the different layers of the project  

This led to the creation of **Unified Companion Maps** — a set of documents and diagrams that explained:

- how NDH’s layers fit together  
- how ideas move safely between them  
- and how users can navigate the system without overwhelm  

Surfboard stabilized the system.  
Companion Maps **organized** it.

---

## **5. Verdant Deep and the Snek Ecology (public‑facing)**

One of the most surprising parts of NDH’s evolution was the emergence of an expressive ecology — especially the “sneks.”

Sneks began as playful metaphors, but they grew into:

- symbols of safety  
- tools for decompression  
- ways to soften complex ideas  
- and a shared language for emotional clarity  

Verdant Deep — a creative environment inside NDH — gave these expressive elements a home.

This wasn’t frivolous.  
It was essential.

Human‑centered systems need:

- comfort  
- clarity  
- and emotional grounding  

Sneks provided that.

---

## **6. How Trauma‑Informed‑Systems‑Design unified everything**

Eventually, NDH needed a single place where:

- stability tools  
- expressive ecology  
- governance rules  
- and research methods  

could live together.

This became the **Trauma‑Informed‑Systems‑Design** repository.

TISD didn’t replace NDH.  
It **organized** it.

It provided:

- clear boundaries  
- safe design rules  
- consistent governance  
- and a unified home for all the tools NDH had created  

This is why the commit count jumped from ~5,200 to **6,981** — consolidation produces commits.
