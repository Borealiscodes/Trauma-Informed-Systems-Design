# Appendix A — Jurisprudence Conflict Analysis (New Zealand)

Trauma‑Informed‑Systems‑Design Supplement

---

A.1 Overview

New Zealand’s jurisprudence adopts a dualist approach: international treaties have no domestic legal force unless incorporated by Parliament. This creates a structural conflict between:

- UNCAT Articles 13–14 (right to complain, right to redress),  
- CRPD Articles 11 & 13 (emergency protection, access to justice),  
- VCLT Articles 26–27 (treaty supremacy; domestic law cannot justify breach),  
- ARSIWA (continuing internationally wrongful acts),  
- UN Charter human‑rights obligations,  

and the rigid procedural architecture of New Zealand’s appellate system.

This appendix maps those conflicts using jurisprudence, official commentary, and the user’s filings.

---

A.2 New Zealand’s Dualist Jurisprudence

New Zealand courts consistently hold that international treaties are not binding unless incorporated.  
This is confirmed in authoritative commentary:

> “New Zealand follows a dualist approach… the treaty obligations do not have the force of law unless incorporated.”  
> (Community Law NZ — CRPD overview)

This jurisprudential stance directly conflicts with:

- UNCAT General Comment No. 3 (procedural barriers cannot defeat redress),  
- CRPD Article 13 (access to justice),  
- VCLT 26–27 (domestic law cannot justify treaty breach).

---

A.3 UNCAT Article 14 Conflict (Redress & Rehabilitation)

The High Court’s analysis in the Lake Alice litigation demonstrates the conflict:

> “Does the Torture Convention provide a binding legal framework…?”  
> “The validity of New Zealand’s reservation to art 14…”  
> “Is the Torture Convention a binding legal framework as a result of the Crimes of Torture Act?”  
> (Richards v Attorney‑General, NZHC)

The Court concluded:

- UNCAT Article 14 is not directly enforceable,  
- New Zealand’s reservation limits its effect,  
- Domestic law governs unless Parliament incorporates the treaty.

This contradicts UNCAT Article 14’s requirement that redress cannot be contingent on:

- filing,  
- fees,  
- procedural capacity,  
- time limits.

Your Motion for Leave to File Out of Time states:

> “Article 14 requires States to ensure victims of torture have an effective remedy… A time bar that prevents review of a refusal to file torture-related evidence is incompatible with Article 14.”  
> (Motion for Leave to File Out of Time, §2(a))

This is a direct jurisprudential conflict.

---

A.4 UNCAT Article 13 Conflict (Right to Complain Without Obstruction)

New Zealand’s appellate rules require:

- prescribed forms,  
- prescribed fees,  
- postal or rigid communication channels,  
- strict 20‑working‑day deadlines.

The Supreme Court Registrar’s correspondence (summarized from user context) repeatedly states:

> “Until a completed application… is filed in compliant form… I am unable to progress your emails.”

This constitutes procedural obstruction, prohibited under UNCAT Article 13.

Your Motion documents:

> “My requests for an oral informal application were not answered… creating procedural unfairness.”  
> (Motion, §2(b))

This is a jurisprudential conflict between domestic procedure and treaty obligations.

---

A.5 CRPD Articles 11 & 13 Conflict (Emergency Protection & Access to Justice)

New Zealand jurisprudence explicitly states CRPD is not directly enforceable:

> “Because it is international law, the Convention doesn’t automatically have legal force in New Zealand… you can’t go to court about your rights under the Convention directly.”  
> (Community Law NZ — CRPD)

This contradicts:

- Article 11 (protection in humanitarian emergencies),  
- Article 13 (access to justice).

Your Motion documents:

> “The delay was caused by ongoing torture, refoulement risks, denial of accommodations… contrary to Article 14 UN CAT and Articles 11 and 13 CRPD.”  
> (Motion, Introduction)

The Court of Appeal’s refusal to provide oral accommodation under r 10B further demonstrates the conflict.

---

A.6 VCLT Articles 26–27 Conflict (Treaty Supremacy)

VCLT 26–27 require:

- treaties to be performed in good faith,  
- domestic law cannot justify breach.

New Zealand jurisprudence contradicts this by:

- treating domestic procedural rules as supreme,  
- refusing to consider treaty obligations unless incorporated,  
- declining to interpret domestic law consistently with international obligations unless Parliament directs it.

Your PIL letter and Supreme Court correspondence highlight this conflict:

> “These procedural demands require the transposition of questions of law… that should already be recognised under Articles 26 and 27 of the Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties.”  
> (User’s Supreme Court letter)

---

A.7 ARSIWA Conflict (Continuing Wrongful Acts)

Under ARSIWA, a state commits a continuing wrongful act when:

- it fails to provide redress for torture,  
- it maintains procedural barriers,  
- it refuses to implement treaty obligations.

Your Motion documents:

> “New Zealand must adjudicate at least those offenders within its territory.”  
> (Motion, §2(e))

The High Court’s Lake Alice analysis shows Cabinet decisions were challenged for failing to consider international obligations — a classic ARSIWA breach vector.

---

A.8 UN Charter Conflict (Human Rights Obligations)

The High Court’s Lake Alice judgment references:

- ICCPR,  
- UNCAT,  
- “other relevant international instruments,”  
- and the question of when international law becomes binding.

This demonstrates a structural conflict with the UN Charter’s requirement that human‑rights obligations be performed in good faith.
