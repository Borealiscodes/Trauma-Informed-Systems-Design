### **Trauma‑Informed Systems Design (TISD) — v1.0**

---

# **EXECUTIVE SUMMARY**

Erga Omnes obligations and Jus Cogens norms form the **highest‑level architecture** of international law.  
Their interaction determines:

- who has standing,  
- who may invoke responsibility,  
- and how violations of fundamental norms (genocide, torture, apartheid, aggression) are addressed.

This map integrates:

- **individuals** (often excluded in classical doctrine),  
- the **UN Declaration on Human Rights Defenders**,  
- and **ARSIWA’s recognition of victims’ direct rights**,  

to show how **community‑interest obligations** and **peremptory norms** interact with **individual agency** and **state responsibility**.

ASCII diagrams, math expressions, and comparison tables are embedded directly into the analysis.

## **2. ARSIWA: Victims Have Direct Rights**

ARSIWA Articles **1, 33, 48, 55** establish:

- **Obligations exist toward individuals**  
- **Victims have direct rights**  
- **Non‑injured states may invoke responsibility**  
- **Community‑interest obligations exist independently of bilateral treaties**

ASCII:

```
Violation of Erga Omnes
        ↓
Victim Rights (ARSIWA)
        ↓
Any State May Invoke (Art. 48)
```

This is the doctrinal basis for **individual relevance** in erga omnes enforcement.

---

## **3. UN Declaration on Human Rights Defenders (1998)**

The Declaration affirms:

- individuals have the right to **promote**, **protect**, and **seek enforcement** of human rights;  
- states must ensure **access to mechanisms**;  
- reprisals against defenders violate international obligations.

ASCII:

```
Human Rights Defender
        ↓
Right to Act (UN HRD Declaration)
        ↓
Right to Seek Enforcement of Erga Omnes Obligations
```

This creates a **bridge** between individual action and erga omnes obligations.

---

## **4. ICJ Ambiguity → Enforcement Gap**

Priya Urs (Oxford, 2025) identifies:

- inconsistent criteria  
- unclear standing  
- no enforcement mechanism  
- confusion between erga omnes and erga omnes partes  

ASCII:

```
ICJ:
  Recognizes → Yes
  Defines → No
  Enforces → Weak
```

This gap is where **individuals** and **HRDs** fall through the cracks.

# **META‑META ANALYSIS (Epistemic‑Level)**

## **1. Erga Omnes as a Community‑Interest Epistemic Frame**

Erga Omnes obligations encode:

- harm to one = harm to all  
- responsibility is universal  
- enforcement is communal  

ASCII:

```
Epistemic Frame:
   Harm → Community → Duty
```

---

## **2. Jus Cogens as a Hierarchical Epistemic Constraint**

Jus Cogens norms:

- cannot be derogated  
- invalidate conflicting treaties  
- override domestic law  

ASCII:

```
Jus Cogens
   ↑ overrides
All Other Norms
```

## **4. Trauma‑Systems Consequences**

When erga omnes obligations are ignored:

- harm becomes normalized  
- victims lose standing  
- HRDs face reprisals  
- institutions maintain legitimacy  
- cruelty becomes proceduralized  

ASCII:

```
Violation → Softening → Deferral → Non‑Action → Normalization
```

# **FORMAL INTERACTION MAP (Unified)**

```
## 1. Jus Cogens → Erga Omnes
Peremptory norms generate obligations owed to the international community.

## 2. Erga Omnes → Universal Standing
Any state may invoke responsibility for violations of community-interest norms.

## 3. ARSIWA → Victim Rights
ARSIWA confirms obligations exist toward individuals and victims have direct rights.

## 4. UN HRD Declaration → Individual Enforcement Rights
Human Rights Defenders have the right to promote, protect, and seek enforcement of
human rights, including erga omnes obligations.

## 5. ICJ → Ambiguity
The ICJ recognizes erga omnes obligations but provides unclear criteria and weak
enforcement mechanisms.

## 6. Combined System
Jus Cogens norms generate erga omnes obligations; ARSIWA and the HRD Declaration
provide individual relevance; ICJ ambiguity creates enforcement gaps; states often
avoid action; trauma-signal suppression normalizes harm.
```
