# VIGNETTE III — The Collapse of Domestic Remedies (New Zealand)
Witness vs. Judicial Bureaucracy (Third Altitude)

The third altitude of harm is the quietest.

No confrontation.  
No hearing.  
No oral submissions.  
No opportunity to speak.

Just silence — formal, procedural, and ongoing.

By the time I reached the judicial layer, the academic and municipal altitudes had already failed.  
But in New Zealand, the collapse began even earlier — with the national human rights institution.

---

The New Zealand Human Rights Commission (NZHRC)
The Administrative Altitude Before the Judicial One

On 18 July 2025, the New Zealand Human Rights Commission informed me:

> “We will only consider written communication received by post…”

This restriction was imposed before the Commission ever acknowledged my original complaint.

I had submitted a discrimination complaint under:

- disability,  
- political opinion,  
- victimisation,  
- and torture‑related procedural harm.

But the Commission never confirmed receipt.  
Never opened the complaint.  
Never processed it.  
Never engaged with it.  
Never provided accommodations.  
Never recognized my disability.  
Never recognized my status as a human rights defender.  
Never recognized the duress under which I was communicating.  
Never recognized the torture evidence.  
Never recognized the emergency declaratory judgment context.

Instead, they imposed a postal‑only restriction because my communications reflected duress as a neurodivergent human rights defender, not because they were improper.

The restriction was not a response to misconduct.  
It was a response to disability‑related communication under torture‑related stress.

And then — on 24 July 2026 — they extended it:

> “Considering the frequency of emails over the last 12 months, we find it necessary to maintain the restrictions… for a further 12 months.”

The extension occurred:

- without reviewing the original complaint,  
- without acknowledging the original complaint,  
- without providing disability accommodations,  
- without recognizing the emergency declaratory judgment context,  
- without recognizing the torture evidence,  
- without recognizing the nemo judex conflict,  
- without recognizing the systemic collapse of domestic remedies.

The NZHRC did not merely fail to act.  
It institutionalized avoidance.

When the NZHRC extends a blacklisting without ever acknowledging the original complaint, the courts inherit a vacuum.

---

The High Court of New Zealand

I did not arrive at the High Court passively.

I submitted:

- multiple Interlocutory Applications,  
- a Declaratory Judgment request,  
- requests for oral hearing,  
- supporting evidence,  
- international law citations,  
- documentation of torture,  
- documentation of procedural collapse,  
- requests for disability accommodations.

Each filing was lawful.  
Each filing was procedurally correct.  
Each filing was grounded in evidence.

But the Registrar refused to accept them.

Not angrily.  
Not dramatically.  
Just procedurally.

“There is no basis for filing this.”  
“This cannot be accepted.”  
“This is not the correct route.”

The refusal was administrative.  
But its effect was structural.

A court cannot protect a person whose filings it refuses to receive.

---

The Court of Appeal of New Zealand

The next step was automatic — because the High Court had closed its doors.

I again requested:

- recognition of filings,  
- oral hearing,  
- disability accommodations,  
- procedural access.

But the Court of Appeal proceeded without me.

Not because I refused to attend.  
Not because I was unwilling.  
But because I was never given a hearing.

The judgment — [2026] NZCA 476 — was delivered entirely on the papers, despite:

- my explicit request for an oral hearing,  
- my documented disability,  
- CRPD Article 13 obligations,  
- the nemo judex conflict,  
- the torture evidence,  
- the emergency declaratory judgment context.

The Court of Appeal did not need to silence me.  
It only needed to proceed without me.

---

The Supreme Court of New Zealand
(Hopeful — with a Grain of Salt)

The final domestic remedy is the Supreme Court of New Zealand.

I submitted:

- notice of appeal,  
- supporting evidence,  
- requests for oral hearing,  
- requests for disability accommodations,  
- documentation of lower‑court procedural collapse,  
- documentation of torture,  
- documentation of international law obligations.

And the Leave to Appeal was submitted at 3:00 AM IST today.

It is too early for silence to mean deflection.  
Too early for omission to be interpreted as refusal.  
Too early for the pattern to repeat.

Right now, the Supreme Court’s silence is simply the first hours of a process.

There is genuine possibility —  
that the Court may acknowledge,  
may intervene,  
may break the pattern,  
may provide the hearing that every lower court denied.

The hope is real.

But it carries a grain of salt.

Because the NZHRC did not merely blacklist me —  
they extended the blacklisting.

Because every prior layer of New Zealand bureaucracy has produced deflection.  
Because every prior attempt at remedy has been met with omission.  
Because every prior request for hearing has been ignored.

So the hope is present — but informed.  
Alive — but cautious.  
Open — but shaped by experience.

I am hopeful.  
Truly hopeful.  
But I know the pattern well enough to keep a grain of salt close at hand.

---

The Pattern

Across all New Zealand judicial and quasi‑judicial bodies, the pattern has been the same:

- filings not accepted,  
- interlocutory applications not processed,  
- declaratory judgment not acknowledged,  
- oral hearings not granted,  
- accommodations not provided,  
- procedures not adapted,  
- remedies not accessible.

And now, at the highest level, there is a moment of possibility —  
a pause that could become remedy,  
or could become another deflection.

---

The Realization

With the judicial layer unfolding in real time, the pattern is nearly complete:

Academic bureaucracy punishes witness.  
Municipal bureaucracy erases identity.  
Judicial bureaucracy erases remedy —  
unless the Supreme Court chooses to break the pattern.

None of it requires force.  
None of it requires hostility.  
None of it requires intent.

It requires only procedure.

And that procedure — polite, rigid, and unyielding — becomes the third tale of grounding:

Bureaucracy can deny a person justice without ever raising its voice —  
and sometimes, it leaves a small window open for hope.
