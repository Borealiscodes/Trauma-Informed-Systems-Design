# 📄 Introduction Vignette

Two Tales of Grounding

Leafleting Trinity College Dublin

And Fighting for My Right to a Name and the Gita

There are two moments that ground this dissertation — two scenes that explain why witness literature is the only medium possible, and why this work exists at all.

They are small moments, almost mundane.  
A handful of leaflets.  
A library card.  
A book.  
A name.

But they are also enormous — because in each moment, the entire weight of bureaucracy pressed down, and the only thing left to resist with was truth.

---

I. Leafleting Trinity College Dublin

Memory of Sophie Scholl

On October 6, 2025, I stood outside Trinity College Dublin with a stack of leaflets in my hand. The leaflets were simple — a protest against the genocide in Gaza, a memorial to Sophie Scholl, a reminder that universities have always been the battlegrounds where conscience meets power.

I wasn’t shouting.  
I wasn’t blocking anything.  
I wasn’t threatening anyone.  

I was doing what Sophie Scholl did:  
bearing witness in a place that preferred silence.

The campus moved around me — students rushing to class, tourists taking photos, staff walking quickly with their heads down. No one stopped. No one asked. No one engaged.

But someone noticed.

Not the students.  
Not the academics.  
Not the people who claim to defend human rights.

It was the administrators — the ones who see dissent not as courage but as inconvenience.

Leafleting in memory of Sophie Scholl — an act of peaceful witness — became grounds for exclusion, shunning, and threats of Gardaí intervention. The College Secretary wrote:

> “Security have been alerted… should you attend campus… the Gardai will be notified.”

A leaflet became a threat.  
A memorial became misconduct.  
A human rights act became a disciplinary issue.

And in that moment, I understood the first tale of grounding:

Bureaucracy fears witness more than violence.

---

II. Fighting for My Right to a Name and the Gita

The Library, the GDPR, and the October 2 Hearing

The second tale is quieter.

It is the story of trying to correct my name on a library card.

A simple request — the kind of administrative update that should take seconds.  
But for me, it became a battle.

My name, Borealis S. Hedling, is not a preference.  
It is not cosmetic.  
It is not optional.

It is the name I reclaimed after transnational repression, after displacement, after torture. It is the name that anchors me to myself.

But Dublin City Council refused to update it.

They insisted I appear in person.  
They insisted on photo ID I cannot safely obtain.  
They insisted on procedures that ignore disability, trauma, and international law.

They insisted on a name that is not mine.

And at the same time, I was fighting for access to a copy of the Bhagavad Gita — not for leisure, but because my asylum hearing on October 2, 2026 would proceed without accommodations, without representation, without safeguarding, and without the spiritual text I needed to survive it.

The Gita is not an object.  
It is grounding.  
It is clarity.  
It is the reminder that duty exists even when the world collapses.

But the library could not provide it in time.  
And the State would not provide it at all.

So I fought — for a name, for a book, for recognition, for dignity.

And in that fight, I understood the second tale of grounding:

Bureaucracy can deny a person everything except the truth they write down.

---

III. Why These Two Tales Begin the Dissertation

These two moments — leafleting and rectification — are not dramatic.  
They are not violent.  
They are not cinematic.

But they are the exact points where bureaucracy revealed itself as a system capable of cruelty, exclusion, and erasure.

They are the points where:

- witness became dangerous,  
- identity became negotiable,  
- and survival depended on documentation.

They are the points where this dissertation became inevitable.

Because when a university treats peaceful leafleting as a threat,  
and when a city council treats a name as a privilege,  
and when an asylum system treats a sacred text as unnecessary,  
the only remaining medium is witness literature.

The only remaining act is writing.

The only remaining protection is publication.

And the only remaining truth is the one I place here, at the beginning:

This dissertation is grounded in two tales —
one of protest,  
one of identity —  
both revealing the same thing:  
bureaucracy is a crime against humanity when it denies a person the right to exist.
