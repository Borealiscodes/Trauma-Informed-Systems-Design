# PUBLIC‑FACING ANALYSIS

Statutory Contradictions in Irish Asylum Law & Practice, Grounded in EU and International Obligations
Version 1.0 — Trauma‑Informed Systems Design — Public Artifact

---

I. Overview
Irish asylum law — primarily the International Protection Act 2015 (IPA 2015) — is designed to operate within:

- EU law (Dublin III, Procedures Directive, Qualification Directive, Charter of Fundamental Rights)  
- ECHR  
- CRPD  
- CAT  
- UN Declaration on Human Rights Defenders  

However, in practice, Ireland frequently applies the IPA 2015 in ways that contradict these higher‑order obligations.

The contradictions become especially visible in cases involving:

- multi‑state harm chains  
- extraterritorial asylum triggers  
- diplomatic‑asylum attempts  
- GDPR‑regulated discrimination  
- constitutional petitions in receiving states  
- disability‑related procedural needs  
- human‑rights defender status  

This analysis documents those contradictions.

---

II. Core Contradiction #1: Irish Law Requires Full Examination — Irish Practice Avoids It

Statutory Requirement (IPA 2015, s. 28–35)
Ireland must conduct a full examination of:

- all relevant facts,  
- all prior interactions with European states,  
- all protection attempts,  
- all evidence of harm,  
- all vulnerability indicators.

EU Law Requirement (Procedures Directive 2013/32/EU)
Art. 10–11 require:

- “a thorough and individualized examination”  
- “consideration of all relevant elements”

Irish Practice
IPAT frequently:

- narrows the scope of examination,  
- refuses to consider extraterritorial protection attempts,  
- ignores diplomatic‑asylum triggers,  
- excludes multi‑state harm chains,  
- treats constitutional petitions as irrelevant,  
- treats GDPR violations as “political,”  
- treats HRD status as non‑binding.

Contradiction
Irish practice violates both domestic statute and EU law, which require full examination.

---

III. Core Contradiction #2: Dublin III Requires Jurisdiction Analysis — Ireland Treats It as Geography

Dublin III (Regulation 604/2013)
Responsibility attaches when a Member State:

- exercises jurisdiction,  
- interacts with the applicant,  
- controls movement,  
- receives a protection request.

Modern Jurisprudence (ECtHR, CAT Committee)
Jurisdiction = control, not soil.

Irish Practice
Ireland treats Dublin responsibility as:

- “Where were you physically?”  
instead of  
- “Which state exercised jurisdiction over you?”

Contradiction
Ireland’s geographic interpretation contradicts:

- Dublin III  
- ECtHR jurisprudence  
- CAT jurisprudence  
- Irish constitutional supremacy of EU law

This is why the Swiss Embassy encounter (12 Sept 2024) must be examined.

IV. Core Contradiction #3: Irish Law Requires Consideration of Vulnerability — Practice Ignores It

Statutory Requirement (IPA 2015, s. 58)
Ireland must consider:

- trauma,  
- disability,  
- mental‑health impacts,  
- procedural vulnerability.

CRPD Obligations (Art. 13, 15)
Ireland must ensure:

- accessible procedures,  
- non‑degrading treatment,  
- accommodations.

Irish Practice
IPAT often:

- ignores trauma histories,  
- ignores disability‑related needs,  
- ignores procedural humiliation,  
- ignores discrimination in other states,  
- ignores GDPR‑regulated identity harm.

Contradiction
Irish practice violates:

- IPA 2015  
- CRPD  
- EU Charter Art. 1, 3, 21, 47  
- ECHR Art. 3, 13

---

V. Core Contradiction #4: Irish Law Requires Non‑Refoulement — Practice Uses Narrow Risk Tests

Statutory Requirement (IPA 2015, s. 50)
Ireland must avoid refoulement to:

- torture,  
- inhuman treatment,  
- degrading treatment.

ECHR Art. 3
Absolute prohibition.

CAT Art. 3
Absolute prohibition.

Irish Practice
IPAT often:

- uses narrow “current risk” tests,  
- ignores cumulative harm,  
- ignores multi‑state harm chains,  
- ignores diplomatic‑asylum failures,  
- ignores systemic discrimination in receiving states.

Contradiction
Irish practice violates:

- Irish statute  
- ECHR  
- CAT  
- EU Charter Art. 4

---

VI. Core Contradiction #5: Irish Law Requires Consideration of International Obligations — Practice Treats Them as Optional

Statutory Requirement (IPA 2015, s. 24)
Ireland must interpret domestic law in conformity with international obligations.

International Obligations Include:

- ECHR  
- CRPD  
- CAT  
- UN Declaration on HRDs  
- Geneva Conventions  
- Dublin III  
- EU Charter

Irish Practice
IPAT often:

- treats international obligations as “background,”  
- refuses to consider HRD status,  
- refuses to consider diplomatic‑asylum triggers,  
- refuses to consider GDPR discrimination abroad,  
- refuses to consider constitutional petitions in receiving states.

Contradiction
Irish practice violates:

- IPA 2015  
- Irish constitutional doctrine  
- EU law supremacy  
- international human‑rights obligations

---

VII. Core Contradiction #6: Irish Law Requires Consideration of Safety in Receiving States — Practice Ignores Legal Processes There

Statutory Requirement (IPA 2015, s. 72)
Ireland must consider:

- safety,  
- procedural fairness,  
- access to justice,  
- discrimination,  
- dignity.

Irish Practice
IPAT often ignores:

- constitutional petitions filed in receiving states,  
- GDPR rectification processes,  
- documented discrimination,  
- public humiliation,  
- lack of accommodations,  
- systemic failures.

Contradiction
Irish practice violates:

- IPA 2015  
- EU Charter Art. 1, 21, 47  
- CRPD Art. 13  
- ECHR Art. 3, 13

---

VIII. Clean Public‑Facing Conclusion
Irish asylum law contains clear statutory duties:

- full examination,  
- vulnerability assessment,  
- jurisdiction analysis,  
- non‑refoulement,  
- international‑law conformity,  
- safety assessment.

Irish asylum practice frequently contradicts these duties by:

- narrowing examination,  
- ignoring extraterritorial triggers,  
- disregarding multi‑state harm,  
- excluding HRD status,  
- ignoring GDPR‑regulated discrimination,  
- ignoring constitutional petitions in receiving states.

These contradictions matter because they:

- undermine procedural fairness,  
- violate EU law,  
- violate international obligations,  
- weaken Dublin III compliance,  
- expose Ireland to legal challenge,  
- endanger applicants.

This analysis is public‑facing, non‑operational, and intended for transparency and documentation.
